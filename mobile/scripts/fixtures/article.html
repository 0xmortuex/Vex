How tides work

By Ada Marsh · 1 October 2026

Tides are the regular rise and fall of the sea. They are caused mostly by the Moon's gravity pulling on the oceans, with the Sun adding a smaller pull of its own.

Two bulges, two tides

The Moon pulls hardest on the water nearest to it, making a bulge. On the far side of the Earth the water is pulled least, and a second bulge forms there. As the Earth turns, most coasts pass through both bulges every day, which is why there are usually two high tides and two low tides.

Spring and neap tides

When the Sun and Moon line up, at new and full moon, their pulls add together and the tides are largest: these are spring tides. When they pull at right angles, at the quarter moons, the tides are smallest: neap tides.

The tide is the ocean's pulse, beating twice a day.

Local coastlines matter too. A long narrow bay can funnel the tide into a range of many metres, while an enclosed sea like the Mediterranean barely notices it.
